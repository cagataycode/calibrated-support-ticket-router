Calibrated support ticket router

Jev Ticket Router

Whoever files a ticket shouldn't have to pick the right team. They just describe the problem. An AI model reads the ticket, and simple, visible rules send it to the right department's inbox. When the AI isn't sure, a person decides.

How it works

1Someone writes a ticketTitle and description. No category to choose.
2Jev answers small questions18 quick yes/no and multiple-choice questions, all at once, in about 0.2 seconds.
3Rules decidePlain rules turn the answers into an inbox, copies to other teams, a priority and labels.
4Inbox or a personClear cases are routed. Unsure cases go to a triage inbox for a person to decide.

Jev is an AI model from TypeSafe. Unlike a chatbot, it doesn't write text. It answers typed questions with a probability, such as "billing: 94% likely". That probability is what lets the rules tell sure cases from unsure ones.

Results

We tested on 100 support tickets from a public dataset. We hand-labelled the correct team for each one, and we compared Jev with Claude Opus 5.5, a leading general-purpose AI model, given the same list of teams.

Can you trust Jev's confidence?

Yes. When Jev says it's 98% sure, it's right 97% of the time. This is what makes the triage inbox work: low confidence really does mean "check this".

What we learned

FindingEvidence
The public dataset's own labels were mostly wrongAgainst them, both models scored under 30%. Where Jev and Claude agreed with each other, the label matched only 34% of the time. One example: a projector problem filed under HR.
The dataset's 10 "departments" overlappedThey mixed three questions: which team owns it, what kind of request it is, and who is asking. Real helpdesks keep these separate.
Real-world teams made routing far clearerAfter switching to 10 real teams, Jev and Claude agreed on 86% of tickets, up from 58%. Tickets routed without a person rose from 68% to 90%.
Most mistakes sit on real team boundariesHow-to questions about security, whether the writer runs the servers or only uses them, and billing versus sales. These were the same tickets that were hard for a person to label.
Splitting "urgency" in two fixed odd priorities"How many people are blocked" and "is money or data being lost right now" are separate questions. A broken office heater no longer ranks with a data leak for the wrong reason.

What Jev is asked

Jev never answers "where should this go?" directly. It answers small questions a person could answer at a glance, and the rules make the decision. That keeps the decision visible and easy to change.

The rules

If…Then…
No team fits, or Jev is unsure which team owns it (below 60%)Send to triage, with the top two suggestions for the person
OtherwiseSend to the top team
Another team clearly has work in it (above 70%)Send that team a copy
Security is involvedPriority at least high, copy to Security
Money or data is being lost right nowPriority highest
It might be a confidential personal matterMark restricted, no copies
The AI service failsTreat it as an error and send to triage, never as a "no"

"Yes" means above 70%, "no" means below 30%, and anything in between counts as unsure.

The prompt used for Claude

For a fair comparison, Claude got the same company description and team list that Jev uses, and had to pick exactly one team.

Show the full prompt 
You route support tickets for Northwind: A technology company that sells cloud software (project management, data analytics, marketing tools) and related IT services to business customers. Customers email the helpdesk, and employees use the same ticket system for internal requests.
Pick the one department that should own the ticket.

Departments:
- customer_support: First-line help for customers when nothing is broken: how-to questions, how to set up or integrate a product, requests for documentation, account questions, help getting better results, and general questions about the company.
- engineering: The product team: bugs, crashes, errors, wrong results, slow performance and failing integrations in our products as a user experiences them, plus requests for new features or changes to a product.
- operations: The team that runs our servers and services: outages, service interruptions, planned maintenance, and server capacity or overload. Also problems reported by someone who operates the system (restarted servers, checked logs, tuned databases) rather than someone who only uses it.
- security: Data breaches, leaks, unauthorized access, phishing and malware, and requests to strengthen security, encryption or data protection.
- billing: Invoices, charges, payments, refunds, subscriptions, and questions about payment options or fees for something already bought.
- sales: People who want to buy something new: questions about what we offer, quotes, demos, upgrades to a bigger plan, and requests for new paid services or projects.
- internal_it: Our own employees' laptops, accounts, passwords, VPN, email and office network. Not problems in the products customers use.
- hr: Employees and job candidates: pay, expenses, leave, contracts, workplace policies, training programmes, hiring, and complaints about a colleague.
- legal: Contracts and terms, data-protection (GDPR) requests from customers, NDAs, and legal threats.
- facilities: The office building: heating, desks, cleaning, keys and access cards, meeting rooms, and deliveries.

[Then the ticket: "Subject: …" and the body. The answer must be one team name.]

Limits to keep in mind

  • The answer key leans toward Claude. The labels were reviewed from Claude's suggestions (94 of 100 accepted). Jev still scored higher, and on the 6 tickets where the labeller disagreed with Claude, Jev got 4 right and Claude 2.
  • Small, synthetic sample. 96 scored tickets from a generated dataset. Real company tickets are needed before trusting the numbers.
  • The team descriptions were written after reading these tickets, which may flatter both models.
  • Vague tickets slip through. All 4 tickets the labeller marked "unclear" were routed automatically instead of going to a person. Fix planned: treat "unsure if there's enough detail" as "needs info".
  • Data leaves the company. Jev runs only on TypeSafe's servers, so tickets with personal details are sent outside. Real use needs a data agreement and removal of personal data first.
